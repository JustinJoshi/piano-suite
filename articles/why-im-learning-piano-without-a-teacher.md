# Why I'm Learning Piano Without a Teacher

I started piano because I wanted to understand jazz. Not to perform, not to audition — just to sit at a keyboard and know what I was hearing. The problem was immediate: private lessons run $60 an hour and up, and that was not in the budget. So I did what a lot of self-taught pianists do. I bought a cheap MIDI keyboard, opened Anki, and started collecting YouTube explanations like they were receipts.

That worked, sort of. I could name chord qualities and spell voicings on paper. But naming a chord is not the same as playing it. My hands had no idea where to go. I would look at a lead sheet, think "Cmaj7#11," and then stare at the keys while my brain did inventory. The gap between knowing and doing was wide, and it stayed wide for longer than I want to admit.

## The routine that actually moved things

What eventually helped was separating the two jobs. Anki handled the memory job: *what* chord is this, what notes does it contain, when should I review it again. A physical drill handled the motor job: hands off the keys, chord announced, play it, get timed, repeat until it stops requiring thought. The drill gave me the thing YouTube could not — honest feedback under time pressure.

I built the first version of that drill as a single HTML page. It was ugly, it had no persistence, and it only knew a few chord qualities. But it worked. I started using it every day. Then I wanted a timer. Then I wanted tracking. Then I wanted to drill arpeggios and progressions the same way. The pages multiplied.

## Why Piano Suite exists

Piano Suite is the cleaned-up, shareable version of that messy set of pages. It is the toolkit I wanted when I started: drills, timers, pattern labs, and tracking built to live inside a self-taught routine, with Anki as the other half of it. Everything that runs locally on your machine stays free. The paid tier only covers things that cost real money to run — sync across devices and the live-resonance float panel.

But a toolkit is only part of the answer. The harder part of teaching yourself is staying motivated, knowing what to do next, and avoiding the injuries and burnout that come from practicing wrong. That is why Piano Suite is becoming a community — a place where self-taught pianists learn together, share routines, and shape the tools we all use.

I am not a professional pianist. I am a person building the tool I needed, in public, while I keep learning. The project is open source because the best way to make it useful is to let other self-taught pianists tell me what is broken, what is missing, and what they actually use.

If you are teaching yourself too, questions and ideas are always welcome.

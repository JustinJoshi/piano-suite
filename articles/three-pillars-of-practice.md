Three habits decide whether self-taught practice sticks. None of them are
about talent, and all of them are boring in the best way. This is the short
introduction new pianists see in the app, written out so you can read it at
your own pace.

## 1. Active recall and spaced repetition

The fastest way to waste practice time is to re-read something you already
saw. Looking at a chord chart again feels like studying; it isn't. What
builds memory is retrieval — producing the answer from scratch, then spacing
those retrievals over days.

Retrieval practice roughly doubled week-later retention compared with
re-reading in Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiments. Spaced repetition is
the scheduling half: you review each fact right before you would forget it,
which is far cheaper than re-learning it from zero.

Anki handles the scheduling for you. It tracks what you are about to forget
and shows it to you right before that happens. Use it for chord names,
progressions, and anything else you need to remember cold. Piano Suite's
drills plug into that schedule with Anki Sync, but the motor-memory loop
works even if you pick chords manually.

**In practice:** drill a chord voicing by naming it before you play it, not
by glancing at a chart. Space those drills across days. Let the schedule,
not your mood, decide what you review.

## 2. Take care of yourself

Piano puts your hands under repeated load. If your wrists and tendons are
not supported, practice becomes injury — and injury is the thing that
actually ends practice.

Stretch, rest, and build the muscles around your wrists so they can absorb
that load. A few minutes of hand care is not a break from practice; it is
part of it. Strong, mobile hands let you practice consistently, and
consistency is what makes spaced repetition work.

**In practice:** two minutes of wrist and finger stretches before a session.
Stop when something hurts — sharp pain is information, not weakness.

## 3. Manage your frustrations

Your brain has two modes. Focused mode absorbs new material; diffuse mode,
the wandering one, is where connections settle in the background. You need
both, and the second one only happens when you step away.

Practice in short, focused blocks — twenty minutes of attention, then a real
break. Walk away, breathe, let your mind wander. Consistent short sessions
beat rare marathon sessions, and the diffuse time is when the memory
actually sticks.

**In practice:** set a timer for twenty minutes. When it rings, stop even if
things are going well. The stop is what makes tomorrow's session work.

## The honest summary

None of this is exciting. That is the point: the boring habits are the ones
that compound. A practice page with a metronome, a timer, and a chord target
is just these three pillars turned into something you can press start on.
